§ 1 — Introduction

Why Analyze Systems in Two Domains?

Every physical system — a robot arm, an electric circuit, a mechanical spring — can be studied from two fundamentally different perspectives. Neither is more correct. They are complementary windows into the same physical reality.

Time Domain

"How does the output evolve with time after an input is applied?"

You apply a force, a voltage, or a command — and you watch what happens next. Does the system overshoot? Oscillate? Settle quickly?

Frequency Domain

"How does the system respond to sinusoidal inputs at every frequency?"

You sweep through frequencies and ask: which ones pass through? Which are blocked? By how much? With what delay?

The Transfer Function Bridge

For a linear time-invariant (LTI) system, the transfer function connects input to output in the Laplace domain:

G(s) = Y(s) / U(s)

To obtain the frequency response, substitute s = jω (where j = √−1 and ω is angular frequency in rad/s):

G(jω) = frequency response at angular frequency ω
💡 Key Insight

The Laplace variable s encodes both transient behavior (exponential growth/decay) and oscillatory behavior (via jω). Evaluating G(s) on the imaginary axis s = jω extracts only the steady-state sinusoidal response — this is the frequency domain.

§ 2 — Time Domain Analysis

Observing How Systems Evolve in Time

Time-domain analysis is the most intuitive approach: apply a known input and watch the output unfold. The most common test is the step response — a sudden jump from 0 to a constant value.

🤖 Robotics Example

A robot arm joint is commanded from 0° to 30°. In the time domain, we observe: does it reach 30° quickly? Does it overshoot to 35° and oscillate? Does it creep up slowly? Each behavior reveals damping and responsiveness.

Second-Order System Model

Most physical systems can be approximated as second-order. The canonical transfer function is:

G(s) = ω_n² / (s² + 2ζω_n·s + ω_n²)

where ω_n is the natural frequency (rad/s) and ζ is the damping ratio (dimensionless).

§ 3 — Limitations of Time-Domain Analysis

The Blind Spots of Time-Domain Analysis

Time-domain plots are wonderfully physical and intuitive. But they hide important information:

✗ Frequency selectivity — Which frequencies are amplified or attenuated? Unreadable from a step response.

✗ Noise sensitivity — Does the system amplify sensor noise? High-frequency noise may be invisible in a step test.

✗ Phase lag at frequency — How much does output lag input at 10 Hz? 100 Hz? Unknown from time domain alone.

✗ Stability margins — How close is the feedback loop to instability? Gain and phase margins are frequency-domain concepts.

✗ Resonance peaks — A system may look fine in a step test but hide a dangerous resonance at some frequency.

✗ Bandwidth — The highest trackable frequency is not visible from a single step response.

⚠️ Critical Robotics Example

A robot arm tracks a slow ramp command beautifully in simulation. But in reality, high-frequency vibration from the motor gearbox excites a resonance at 40 Hz, causing violent oscillation. This would never be caught from a step-response test alone — it requires frequency-domain analysis.

§ 4 — Frequency Domain Analysis

Studying How Systems Treat Each Frequency

Instead of asking "what happens after a step?", we ask: "if I feed a pure sinusoid at frequency ω, what does the output look like in steady state?"

Input: u(t) = A · sin(ωt) Output: y(t) = A · |G(jω)| · sin(ωt + ∠G(jω))

For any LTI system, the output is also a sinusoid at the same frequency — but with changed amplitude and shifted phase:

MAGNITUDE
|G(jω)|

Ratio of output to input amplitude. If |G(jω)| > 1, that frequency is amplified. If < 1, it is attenuated.

PHASE
∠G(jω)

Phase shift between input and output. Negative value means output lags behind input — critical for feedback stability.

💡 Why This Matters Deeply

Every real-world signal can be decomposed into sinusoids (Fourier's theorem). If you know how your system treats every sinusoid, you know everything about how it treats every possible input. The frequency response is a complete characterization of an LTI system.

§ 5 — Bode Magnitude Plot

Mapping Gain Across All Frequencies

A Bode magnitude plot displays the gain of a system versus frequency on a log-log scale.

Magnitude in dB = 20 · log₁₀ |G(jω)|
💡 Why decibels?

Gain of 1 (no change) = 0 dB. Gain of 10 = 20 dB. Gain of 0.1 = −20 dB. The log scale compresses large dynamic ranges and makes cascaded gains additive: dB_total = dB_1 + dB_2.

First-Order Low-Pass Example

An RC circuit or mechanical dashpot follows:

G(s) = 1 / (τs + 1) ⟹ G(jω) = 1 / (1 + jωτ)

Corner frequency at ω_c = 1/τ. Below this: signals pass unchanged. Above it: attenuation at −20 dB/decade.

§ 6 — Bode Phase Plot and Stability Margins

Measuring Delay Across Frequencies

The phase plot shows how much the output lags behind the input at each frequency. Phase lag is always negative for stable causal systems (output comes after input).

🤖 Robotics Example — Why Phase Matters

A robot joint controller receives a position sensor signal. If there is 180° of phase lag at the feedback loop's crossover frequency, the corrective action arrives exactly out-of-phase — it makes the error worse, not better. This is how feedback loops go unstable.

Phase Margin — The Stability Buffer

Phase margin (PM) is how many additional degrees of phase lag the system can tolerate before going unstable:

Phase Margin = 180° + ∠G(jω_gc) where |G(jω_gc)| = 1 (0 dB crossover)

PM = 45° is a healthy stability buffer. PM < 20° is dangerously close to instability. PM = 0° means the system is on the verge of sustained oscillation.

💡 Physical Intuition

Phase lag accumulates from every sensor delay, computational delay, actuator lag, and dynamic element in the feedback loop. In modern robotics, even a few milliseconds of computation add phase lag at high frequencies: ΔΦ = −ω · T_delay. This is why real-time control loops run at 1 kHz or faster.

§ 8 — Example 1: RC Circuit

The Same System, Two Perspectives

An RC circuit is the simplest dynamic system with one pole. Let's examine it from both domains.

Time Domain

After a step voltage V₀ is applied, the capacitor charges exponentially:

v_C(t) = V₀(1 − e^(−t/RC))

Time constant τ = RC: after one τ the capacitor is 63% charged; after 5τ it is essentially full.

Frequency Domain

Transfer function and frequency response:

G(s) = 1 / (RCs + 1) G(jω) = 1 / (1 + jωRC)

This is a low-pass filter. Slow signals (low ω) pass through; fast signals (high ω) are blocked.

💡 Same System, Different Stories

In time domain, you see a capacitor charging curve. In frequency domain, you see a filter cutting off high-frequency signals. Both are true descriptions of the same RC circuit. The first is useful for startup behavior; the second reveals noise rejection capability.

§ 9 — Example 2: Mass-Spring-Damper

Mechanical Resonance in Two Domains

The mass-spring-damper is the universal mechanical model — it applies to robot joints, vehicle suspensions, flexible structures, and more.

m·ẍ + c·ẋ + k·x = F(t) G(s) = X(s)/F(s) = 1 / (ms² + cs + k) ω_n = √(k/m) , ζ = c / (2√(km))

📐 Physical Interpretation

When a robot arm applies forces near the natural frequency of a coupled load (e.g., a flexible link or payload on a spring mount), those forces are amplified by resonance. In time domain, this appears as violent oscillation that builds up. Frequency domain reveals the exact danger zone frequency and required damping.

§ 9.5 — Paper Bridge: Input Shaping for Flexible Robot Arms

How the 2025 Input-Shaping Paper Uses the Same Frequency-Domain Idea

The paper “Data Driven Approach to Input Shaping for Vibration Suppression in a Flexible Robot Arm” is a direct continuation of the mass-spring-damper story above. The system is no longer an abstract mass attached to a spring; it is a flexible robot arm whose residual vibration changes across the workspace. The central idea is still the same: identify the vibration frequency, then modify the command so the robot does not excite that mode.

Recent robotics paper — RoboSoft 2025 / arXiv:2506.14405
Data Driven Approach to Input Shaping for Vibration Suppression in a Flexible Robot Arm

The paper treats input shaping as a command-prefiltering method. The desired command is convolved with a short impulse sequence. These impulses are timed so that the elastic wave excited by one part of the command is cancelled by the next part. The novelty is that the shaper parameters are tuned from measured residual-vibration data across the robot workspace, instead of assuming one fixed vibration model everywhere.

Use in this page: Bode resonance peak → vibration frequency estimate → input-shaper timing.

From Bode Peak to Input Shaper

For a lightly damped flexible mode, the transfer function is again approximately second order:

G(s) = ω_n² / (s² + 2ζω_n s + ω_n²)

The Bode magnitude plot shows a resonance peak near ω_n. Input shaping then constructs a modified command:

u_shaped(t) = A₁u(t) + A₂u(t − t_d)

For a zero-vibration shaper, the two weights and delay are chosen as:

K = exp(−ζπ / √(1−ζ²)), A₁ = 1/(1+K), A₂ = K/(1+K), t_d = π / (ω_n√(1−ζ²))
Step 1
Measure vibration

Move the arm and record residual oscillation from motion or sensor data.

Step 2
Estimate the flexible mode

Extract the dominant vibration frequency and damping ratio.

Step 3
Build the input shaper

Choose impulse amplitudes and delay so the flexible response cancels.

Step 4
Interpolate across workspace

Use measured data at several poses to tune the shaper for new poses.

§ 10 — Example 3: Robot Joint Control

Closed-Loop Control of a Motorized Joint

A robot joint is controlled by a motor, position sensor, and feedback controller. This closed-loop system requires both analysis domains.

Time-Domain Questions

→ Does the joint reach the target angle?

→ How much does it overshoot?

→ How long until it settles?

→ Is there steady-state error?

→ Does it follow a trajectory accurately?

Frequency-Domain Questions

→ Can the joint track 10 Hz commands?

→ Does it amplify 200 Hz motor vibration?

→ How much phase lag at the crossover frequency?

→ What are the gain and phase margins?

→ Is it robust to 5 ms computation delay?

💡 The Rule of Thumb in Robotics Control

Time domain validates: "Does the motion look correct?" Frequency domain validates: "Is the controller robust and noise-tolerant?" A production robot controller needs both. A system that passes time-domain tests can still fail catastrophically in the field due to unmodeled resonances or sensor noise amplification.

§ 11 — Modern Robotics and VLA Systems

From Classical Control to Neural Robot Brains

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) systems are a new class of robot controllers where a large neural network — trained on vision, language, and demonstration data — directly produces robot actions from visual observations and natural language instructions.

Examples: Google RT-2, OpenVLA, π₀ from Physical Intelligence. These systems accept plain language: "Pick up the red cup and place it on the plate."

The Control Hierarchy

Level 3 — Semantic / Planning (VLA)
"Pick up the cup." — Vision transformer + language model generates action tokens or waypoints. Operates at ~5–30 Hz. Uses neural networks trained on demonstrations.
Level 2 — Motion Planning
"Move end-effector along this trajectory." — Inverse kinematics, trajectory interpolation, obstacle avoidance. Operates at ~100 Hz. Bridges semantic goal to joint-level commands.
Level 1 — Low-Level Control
"Apply this torque to joint 3 right now." — PID / impedance control at 1–5 kHz. Requires stability margins, bandwidth guarantees, noise rejection. Frequency-domain analysis lives here.
🧠 VLA + Frequency Domain in Practice

A VLA model outputs a wrist position target at 10 Hz. This command must be tracked by the low-level joint controller. The controller's bandwidth must exceed 10 Hz, with adequate phase margin to remain stable under potentially jerky VLA outputs. Frequency-domain tools determine whether the low-level controller can faithfully execute the VLA's intentions without vibration, instability, or lag-induced oscillation.

💡 Delays Are Especially Dangerous in VLA Robots

A large vision-language model may take 50–200 ms to produce an action (inference latency). Every millisecond of delay adds phase lag: ΔΦ = −ω · T_delay. A 100 ms delay at 10 Hz contributes −360° of phase lag — enough to completely destabilize a feedback loop. This is why VLA systems often use open-loop action chunking or separate high-bandwidth reflex layers that bypass the slow neural policy.

§ Final Section — Main Takeaway

The Whole Picture

Time-domain analysis tells us how a system behaves in time — transients, overshoot, settling, and trajectory tracking. It is physical and intuitive.

Frequency-domain analysis tells us how the system treats different frequencies — bandwidth, resonance, noise rejection, phase lag, and stability margins. It reveals what time domain hides.

For robotics, time domain shows whether motion looks correct, while frequency domain reveals robustness, noise sensitivity, bandwidth, delay effects, and stability margins.

Modern VLA robots may use neural networks for perception and decision-making, but their bodies still obey dynamics, delays, bandwidth limits, and feedback stability constraints. Classical control theory remains indispensable at the actuation layer — regardless of how intelligent the brain.
